\begin{abstract}
Flight-plan revisions provide a direct record of route choice: an airline
replaces a route it previously filed with another route for the same
flight. We use 1.48 million such pairs from eighteen months of European
traffic to learn which of two routes the airline is likely to choose.
The contribution is a way to build and evaluate preference models from
these recorded comparisons, without assuming that an unfiled route was
considered and rejected. The model combines route indicators, flight
context and waypoint identifiers, while preventing filing times from
revealing which route came later. On a later, held-out quarter, it
identifies the chosen route in 68.1\,\% of cases, compared with
55.0\,\% for the best single-indicator rule. Accuracy reaches
94.9\,\% on the fifth of pairs with the highest confidence. A smaller
set of matched flights that kept their routes helps the model represent
that behaviour as well. To illustrate how the predictions could guide
fuel-saving proposals, we evaluate a policy that selects the lower-fuel
route only above a calibrated confidence threshold. Of its selected
routes, 79.2\,\% match the airline's subsequent filing. These matches
account for 20.1\,kilotonnes of planned-fuel differences over the quarter.
The results demonstrate useful prediction of recorded route choices;
acceptance of real proposals and additional fuel savings remain questions
for prospective evaluation.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

On a spring morning in 2026, an Airbus A320 was filed from Amsterdam to
Barcelona on a route carrying 102 minutes of attributed air traffic flow
management (ATFM) delay, and the operator subsequently re-filed. The new
route shed that delay completely, and it cost the airline on each of the
three counts it pays for directly: 39 minutes of flight time,
1\,397\,kg of extra planned fuel, and 466 euros more in en-route charges.
The records therefore show a substantial trade-off between attributed
delay and the other route indicators. They do not reveal the operator's
complete reasoning or its internal costs.

This paper asks whether such recorded choices can help predict route
preferences. Airlines revise flight plans for several reasons: to respond
to delay, updated weather, changes in an aircraft's earlier flights, or
route availability. Whatever prompted the change, the archive contains
the route replaced and its replacement. We use those two routes as a
training pair and ask the model to identify the replacement from their
characteristics, without revealing the filing order.

The central methodological choice is how to obtain the training labels.
A common approach ranks a filed route above alternatives generated later.
Those alternatives may be feasible, but the record does not establish
whether the airline considered them. A revision instead supplies two
routes that the airline filed for the same flight. It is direct evidence
of a change in filing, although changing constraints mean that it is not
always a comparison between two options still available at the same time.

The contribution has three parts. First, we construct route comparisons
from revisions while removing information that would reveal the answer
merely through message timing. Secondly, we evaluate both prediction
accuracy and confidence on a later period, and use matched flights that
kept their routes to address the bias towards changes. Thirdly, we examine
which inputs support the predictions and illustrate selective use of the
model in a retrospective fuel-saving proposal policy. The scientific
question is whether these records contain useful, predictable information
about route choice. The proposal policy provides one way to examine the
value of that information.

The remainder of this manuscript is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:background} explains why a pair of routes drawn from a
revision constitutes a preference observation and what a model of such
observations is for, and Section~\ref{sec:related}
positions the work within the literature on rerouting and route choice.
Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the archive, the split protocol and the
model, while Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results. Finally,
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes and outlines future work.

\section{Background} \label{sec:background}

This section explains how a flight-plan revision becomes a labelled
preference pair, why that label is preferred to one built from generated
candidates, and what the model is for.

\subsection{Flight-plan revisions as preference data}

In the European network a flight plan is filed before departure and may be
revised until the flight leaves. Every filing is a message, and so is every later change. The network
keeps them all, so what it holds for a flight is the whole sequence of
messages sent for it. A route change
is accordingly a pair of consecutive messages rather than a record of its
own: the message that changed the route, and the one it
replaced. 

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{pair_idea.pdf}
\caption{Two ways to build a training pair, on one city pair. Left: the
filed route (solid green) is ranked above candidates a route generator
would supply (grey); the grey tracks drawn here are real routes flown on
the same city pair,
standing in for generated ones. Right: a revision supplies two routes that
the same operator filed for the same flight hours apart, one of which it
abandoned. The right-hand pair directly records which filed route replaced the other.}
\label{fig:pairidea}
\end{figure*}

The two together describe a decision, because the operator had
committed to the earlier route and then filed the later one for the same
flight, which makes the later route the one it chose and
the earlier one the route it abandoned.

The sharpest of those decisions are made under flow management. When the
traffic expected in a volume of airspace exceeds what can
safely be handled there, whether because of demand, staffing, weather or
equipment, a \emph{regulation} is placed on it and the flights crossing it are given calculated take-off times at
the rate it can absorb; the difference between a flight's calculated
and requested take-off times is its ATFM delay.

An
operator facing an ATFM delay has a choice that is genuinely
economic: accept it, or file a route that avoids the constrained
volume at the cost of distance, fuel and en-route charges, and neither
answer is universally correct. For instance, a flight with a tight
aircraft rotation, in which one aircraft operates several successive legs,
may find forty minutes of delay far more expensive than a
tonne of fuel, whereas the same delay on the last leg of the day may well be
cheaper than the detour. This is why the decision is worth learning rather
than deriving: the costs that determine it are operator-specific and
flight-specific, and the network does not observe them, though the
outcomes of those trades are recorded. Published European reference
values~\cite{cook2015} give the order of magnitude of the cost of delay
and are widely used for analysis, but they are not the cost function
any particular operator applies.

Seven pairs in ten in this archive carry no regulation on either route
(Table~\ref{tab:composition}), and within those the two routes differ by
a median of one kilogramme of planned fuel.

Using choices to learn preferences follows the idea of revealed
preference~\cite{samuelson1938}. Here the observation is a replacement:
route B was filed after route A for the same flight. We call B the
\emph{chosen} route and A the \emph{abandoned} route throughout the
paper. These terms refer to their roles in the record, not to the scores
later assigned by the model.

An alternative construction, used in the authors' earlier
work~\cite{dalmau2024}, ranks a filed plan above routes supplied by a
generator. Those routes may be feasible, but the record does not show
whether the airline considered them. Labelling each generated route as
less preferred therefore makes an assumption beyond the observed filing.

A revision pair provides a more direct comparison
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pairidea}): the operator filed both routes in succession
for the same flight. The label records which route replaced the other.
It does not establish that a dispatcher manually compared them, or that
the earlier route remained available. We therefore use the recorded
choice as a proxy for preference, rather than as a measurement of the
airline's underlying utility.

With generated alternatives, a model may partly learn to distinguish
filed routes from generated ones. Revision pairs reduce that ambiguity
because both routes passed through the airline's filing process. This is
a reason for the label construction, not a measured comparison of the two
training approaches on identical flights.

\subsection{Potential uses of the predictions}
\label{sec:usecases}

One potential use is to screen fuel-saving route proposals. A model
first ranks two routes; calibration then estimates how often its
higher-ranked route matches the recorded choice. A threshold can retain
only predictions with sufficient confidence. Raising it usually reduces
the number of proposals and increases their precision, defined here as
the fraction that match the airline's subsequent filing. A target
precision is selected on earlier data and measured on later data; it is
not guaranteed by the threshold.

\centering

\label{fig:method}

A second research question is whether predicted route preferences could
help prioritise voluntary rerouting requests when traffic demand exceeds
available capacity. Studying that question would require a joint model
of route acceptability and the effect of each reroute on demand.

Here we evaluate the preference model and simulate the first use with
historical revisions. Actual proposal acceptance and overload release remain outside this
evaluation.

\section{Related work} \label{sec:related}

Work on rerouting in ATFM has largely approached the
problem from the network side. Over two decades ago, the authors of~\cite{bertsimas2000} formulated
rerouting as a dynamic network flow problem, and the authors
of~\cite{mukherjee2009} subsequently added a stochastic model that reroutes
flights as capacity evolves. Both optimise an objective defined by the
network and assume, in effect, that the resulting assignment is executed, so
that the willingness of the airline to fly it is not modelled.

More recently, route choice in Europe was studied as a trade between
fuel and en-route charges~\cite{delgado2015}, which documents an economic aspect of route choice, but uses no label in which the
operator rejected an alternative it had itself filed. The closest work to the present one is that of the authors
of~\cite{murca2018}, who incorporate airline preferences into collaborative
rerouting decisions and show that accounting for them changes which reroutes
are selected. Those preferences, however, are inputs to an optimisation,
whereas this paper learns the preference function itself, at network scale,
from what airlines did rather than from what they stated.
% Cut for space (both are in the journal extension): Airline input already
% reaches flow management in two other places, neither of them route

% which motivates the channel simulated herein but says nothing about
% which proposals would be taken up, and the user-driven prioritisation

The direct predecessor of this work applies learning to rank to flight routes
for a different purpose: predicting the flight plans that have not yet been
filed, so that traffic demand can be estimated earlier than the filing horizon
allows~\cite{dalmau2024}. 

That work established the framing adopted herein, namely a gradient-boosted
ranker that scores routes by their indicators in context, and it reported
the very difficulty that motivates the present paper. Its training pairs rank filed plans above generated alternatives.
Section~\ref{sec:background} explains how revision pairs differ.

\section{Methodology} \label{sec:method}

Figure~\ref{fig:method} separates the five steps of the method. Historical
filings supply labels during training and evaluation. When the model
scores a pair, it receives route characteristics and shared flight
context, not the chosen/abandoned labels. The comparison with the label
is made after scoring. In the diagram's final panel, the probability is
always that of the lower-fuel candidate: if the model ranks that route
second, we use one minus the probability of its first-ranked route.

\subsection{Data and pair construction}

Eighteen consecutive months of European network data, from January 2025 to
June 2026, are used. After retaining only those revisions that change the
horizontal route, and only those pairs with complete key performance
indicators, the archive contains 1.48 million labelled pairs. A pair is one training
example with two rows, one per route. Its label identifies which row is
the replacement; the model must infer that label from the inputs.
Throughout the paper, \emph{planned fuel} means the fuel estimate
attached to the recorded plan, rather than a measurement after the flight.



What those decisions look like matters before any model touches them, and
Table~\ref{tab:composition} breaks the archive down by the regulation
context in which each revision was made.

Each route is described by twenty-three features, summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:features}. The two routes of a pair are separated in time as
well as in geography, however, and that must be addressed before
anything is learned. The replacement is by
construction the later message, so any feature that drifts with time
identifies the answer without saying anything about the quality of the
route. The time remaining to departure is the clearest case: it is
necessarily smaller for the route that replaced the other, so a model given
it in raw form could answer ``the one filed later'', be right on essentially
every pair, and know nothing whatever about routes. This is leakage rather
than learning, and it would be invisible in the accuracy figure.

To ensure a
fair comparison between the two routes, every flight-level attribute was
therefore harmonised across the pair, both routes taking the value carried
by the later message, so that no such attribute can separate them and only
route-level quantities differ. This removes a direct clue to the label;
it does not reconstruct every route indicator at a common earlier
decision time. The filing time is the case that matters
most, and harmonising it in that way leaves both routes carrying the
smaller of the two times to departure, which is what makes the shortcut
disappear.

The four route indicators---flight time, distance, planned fuel and
route charges---are expressed relative to the smaller value in each
pair. For example, planned-fuel values of 2\,000 and 2\,300\,kg become
0 and 300\,kg. This tells the model how much more one option requires,
while flight context describes the journey on which the comparison is
made. It does not assume that every operator values a 300\,kg difference
equally. A zero in the fuel input means ``no extra fuel relative to the
other route'', not a flight using no fuel. Each indicator is normalised
separately: the route with the lowest fuel need not have the lowest
charges, as the example below shows.

both rows receive the later message's value, because a difference in

Waypoint identifiers provide a second description of each route. They
are treated as words in an unordered collection, so the model knows
which published points a route uses but not their order or coordinates.
Two routes can have similar fuel and distance indicators yet pass through
different parts of the network. These identifiers allow the model to
learn associations that the aggregate indicators alone cannot express.

Revision pairs describe flights that changed route. They omit flights
that retained a route despite a delay, so training on revisions alone
may favour alternatives too readily. We add a smaller set of
\emph{stay pairs} to represent the latter behaviour.

A stay pair joins two flights on the same day, with the same origin,
destination and aircraft type. Neither revised its route; one was
regulated and the other was not. The regulated flight's retained route
is labelled preferred, and the other flight's route supplies a comparison.
Unlike a revision pair, this is an inferred label: the record does not
show that the regulated flight considered or could have used that
alternative at its departure time. Both rows are evaluated with the
retained-route flight's context. The comparison therefore asks which
route suits that flight; it does not rank the two flights against each other.

There are 6\,672 stay pairs, including 1\,093 in the test months. They
are split by time and added to the corresponding revision subsets.
During training, their greater group weight increases their contribution
to the ranking loss. Section~\ref{sec:changebias} reports the effect on
both revision and stay-pair accuracy.

The time split separates three tasks. The fitting period teaches the
model how features relate to recorded choices. The tuning period selects
when to stop training, fits the probability mapping and sets the policy
threshold. The final test period measures how those fixed choices perform
on later data. Using different periods matters because a rule that works
on the records used to choose it may perform differently afterwards.

The evaluation is strictly out of time, in the sense that the model is
fitted on January 2025 through February 2026 (1\,134\,064 pairs), tuned on
March 2026 (67\,349), and evaluated once on April to June 2026
(281\,720). Every threshold, calibrator and stopping decision is taken on
the tuning month alone, and flights appearing in the tuning or evaluation
months are removed from the fitting set.

All the data used is held by the
network for operational purposes and was processed under existing
data-handling arrangements. Operator identity enters the model as a
categorical feature only, and no operator or airspace volume is named
anywhere in this paper. The unit of observation is a route pair; a flight
may contribute more than one revision. Pair counts should therefore not
be read as counts of distinct flights.

\subsection{Model, training and calibration}
\label{sec:proposals}

The operational question is comparative: given two routes for one flight,
which of them does the airline prefer? Accordingly, the model gives each
route a single number, which is not a probability and has no units, and which is only meaningful next to the number given to
another route for the same flight. Training pushes those numbers apart in the right direction. For each
labelled pair, a penalty increases when the less-preferred route scores
higher. It falls as the preferred route's lead grows, encouraging the
correct ordering and a larger gap even once the order is right~\cite{burges2005}. Nothing in the
objective asks the model to reproduce a route in isolation, which is what
makes the pair the natural unit.


The scoring function is a CatBoost ensemble of gradient-boosted decision
trees~\cite{prokhorenkova2017}. Each tree adds a correction to the
current score. Shared flight attributes still matter even though they
cannot distinguish the two routes on their own: they can change how the
model interprets a route difference. The trees capture these interactions, such as a flight-time
difference having a different effect when little time remains before
the aircraft's next leg. Tree depth is 8, with the learning rate
selected automatically. Early stopping uses the tuning-period criterion to select how many
trees to retain, rather than continuing training indefinitely. Stay
pairs enter the fitting set with a group weight $w$, swept over
$\{10, 30, 100\}$; Section~\ref{sec:changebias} reports the effect of the adopted weight 10.
% Cut for space (already stated in Sections II-D and IV-A): Key performance
% indicators are normalised within each pair by subtracting the pair minimum.

The scores indicate an ordering, not a probability. For example, scores
of $-2.82$ and $+0.90$ favour the second route by about 3.7 score units.
Calibration maps the size of this gap to an estimated probability that
the higher-scoring route matches the recorded choice. A probability of
0.8 means that, among comparable predictions, about eight in ten are
expected to be correct. For the example's gap of 3.7, the fitted mapping
returns 0.99.

To fit this mapping, the trained model scores pairs from the tuning
month. Their known labels reveal whether each ordering is correct.
Calibration relates the observed correctness to the gap: pairs with
similar gaps supply evidence about how reliable such predictions are.
The resulting mapping is then fixed before the test quarter. This step
changes the probability attached to an ordering; it does not retrain
the route-scoring model.

We compare three mappings on the tuning month: the raw sigmoid of the
gap, a fitted sigmoid (Platt scaling), and a monotone stepwise mapping
(isotonic regression)~\cite{zadrozny2002,guo2017}. Isotonic regression
is constrained not to decrease as the gap grows. It has the lowest
tuning-month log loss, a measure that penalises confident errors, and
is retained for testing.
On the test quarter, the average discrepancy between predicted and
observed rates is 1.8 percentage points for that mapping and 0.9 for
the raw sigmoid. These averages describe calibration across probability
bands; they do not bound the error of every prediction. The test result
is reported without changing the earlier selection.

The retrospective proposal policy uses this probability in three steps.
First, exclude pairs with equal planned fuel; 97.9\,\% remain eligible.
Secondly, identify the lower-fuel route. If it is the higher-scoring
route, use the calibrated probability; otherwise use one minus that probability.
Thirdly, select it only if that probability exceeds $\tau$. The threshold
$\tau=0.600$ was chosen on the tuning month to target 80\,\% precision
and is applied unchanged to the test quarter. A 0.600 probability
threshold and an 80\,\% precision target are different quantities:
the latter is the average correctness of all proposals above the former.

Consider two hypothetical pairs. In the first, the lower-fuel route
has calibrated probability 0.85 of matching the later filing; it passes
the threshold. In the second, the lower-fuel route has probability 0.40;
it is withheld even if its fuel difference is large. The policy therefore
requires both lower planned fuel and sufficient predicted preference.
These illustrative probabilities are not additional experimental results.

This policy compares the two routes without using which was filed first.
It may therefore select either the replacement or the route that was
abandoned. A selection is counted as confirmed only when it matches the
replacement. This is a retrospective test of choice prediction, using an
alternative known from the historical record.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section reports how accurately the model predicts airline choices,
whether the change bias of Section~\ref{sec:method} is controlled, what
the model has learned about route preference, and what the retrospective proposal policy selects.

\subsection{Predictive performance}

On the held-out quarter (April to June 2026, 281\,720 pairs) the model
identifies the airline's chosen route 68.1\,\% of the time (95\,\%
interval 67.9--68.3), against 50\,\% for random choice and 55.0\,\% for
the best single-indicator rule.

No tested single-indicator rule predicts much of the choice. The delay
rule reaches 71.6\,\% where both delay values are available and unequal,
but those pairs represent only 14.6\,\% of the test quarter. The other
pairs are scored as random choices, giving 53.2\,\% overall. Under
this baseline's convention, an unregulated route has a missing delay
value rather than an explicit zero. The rule therefore does not cover
every comparison affected by a regulation. These results support
learning from several inputs jointly.

Not all of those predictions carry the same weight. The model's
confidence in each case is the gap between the scores it assigns to the
two routes: a wide gap means a confident prediction, a narrow gap means
the pair is close to a coin toss. As Section~\ref{sec:proposals}
describes, a calibrator converts that gap into a probability, so that
the model can say not only which route the airline would choose but how
likely it is to be right. On the fifth of
pairs where that confidence is highest, the model is right 94.9\,\% of
the time. Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage} traces the full trade-off: as the
model abstains on low-confidence cases, the share of pairs it answers falls but its accuracy rises. This curve
shows what is gained by withholding uncertain predictions, alongside
the overall accuracy across all pairs.

Revision-only training sees how routes were changed, but not the
alternatives considered when a route was retained. The stay-pair
experiment tests whether adding examples of retention improves the
model's predictions on that second kind of comparison.

A simple rule helps interpret this test. Always preferring the regulated
route would be correct on every stay pair, because that is how the pairs
are constructed. The same rule would be wrong on every revision that
leaves a regulation. Performance must therefore be examined on both
groups, not on stay pairs alone.

The effect is clear on both populations. Trained on revisions alone, the
model scores 68.9\,\% on the revision pairs of the held-out quarter but
only 34.8\,\% on the 1\,093 stay pairs: it usually favours the
alternative over the route that was retained. Adding stay pairs
at group weight~10, meaning each stay pair counts ten times as heavily as
one revision pair in the ranking loss, raises the stay-pair accuracy to
90.0\,\% and brings the revision accuracy to 68.1\,\%, a cost of
0.8~points.
Table~\ref{tab:strata} confirms that the adopted model also scores
88.0\,\% on flights that changed route after a regulation (the
\emph{old route only} stratum), so the correction does not trade one
population for the other. It rules out that fixed regulation-only rule,
while leaving open whether other features reveal how the stay pairs were
constructed. Training and testing stay pairs are from different periods;
the test pairs were never added to the fitting set.

The 10.0\,\% error rate on stay pairs measures how often the model
favours the constructed alternative over the retained route. It is not
a confident-error rate or a measured rejection rate: no confidence
threshold is applied to that figure, and no actual proposal was sent.

To explain the model's scores, we compute Shapley feature attributions
over the held-out quarter~\cite{lundberg2017}. Each attribution assigns
part of a route's score to an input feature. We then compare the feature's
contribution on the two routes, because their score difference determines
the predicted choice.
For example, if a feature contributes $+0.3$ to one route and $+0.1$
to the other, it contributes $0.2$ to their score difference. These
illustrative values explain the calculation, not an additional result.
Figure~\ref{fig:shap} averages the absolute size of such differences
across the test quarter. A large bar means that the feature often
influences this fitted model's comparisons; it is not an estimate of
the airline's economic valuation of that feature.

An ablation removes an input, trains a new model and compares its accuracy
with a model that retains that input. For instance, the 68.2\,\% and
66.8\,\% figures above come from a separate full-scale comparison;
the first is not a different estimate of the main run's 68.1\,\%.
This distinction matters when comparing small differences between runs.

The two numbers answer different questions: attribution measures how much
the feature moves the score of the model that has it, while ablation
measures how much worse a model trained without it would be. Where
features are correlated, as route geometry and cost indicators plainly
are, the model trained without the waypoint text recovers part of the
lost information through the remaining features, so the ablation cost is
smaller than the attribution rank would suggest.

The proposal evaluation asks whether confidence filtering identifies
lower-fuel routes that match recorded choices. Always selecting the
lower-fuel route would match 44.9\,\% of eligible revisions. The model
can improve on that baseline by abstaining when its predicted preference
does not support the lower-fuel option.

Three measures must be kept separate. \emph{Ranking accuracy} asks how
often the higher-scoring route is the recorded replacement across test
pairs. \emph{Proposal precision} asks how often the selected lower-fuel
route is the replacement, only among pairs passing the threshold.
\emph{Proposal coverage} is the fraction of eligible pairs that pass.
The 94.9\,\% accuracy concerns the fifth of pairs with the largest
score gaps, regardless of which route uses less fuel. Proposal precision
instead concerns all lower-fuel candidates whose own probability exceeds
the fixed threshold. A very confident prediction favouring higher fuel
helps the first measure but produces no proposal under the second rule.
Conversely, a less confident lower-fuel prediction can still pass the
0.600 threshold. The two measures therefore describe different subsets.

At the operating point selected to target 80\,\% precision
($\tau=0.600$), the policy selects 56\,162 pairs in the held-out
quarter. Of these selections, 79.2\,\% match the later filing.
For each match, we subtract the lower planned-fuel value from the higher
one; incorrect selections contribute nothing to this sum. The total is
20.1\,kt, equivalent to 63.5\,kt of CO$_2$ at 3.16\,kg per kilogramme
of fuel.

Two annualisation assumptions give 222 to 254\,kt of CO$_2$.
Multiplying the quarter's total by four gives 254\,kt. Applying the
per-eligible-pair rate to an annual eligible volume estimated from the
archive gives 222\,kt. These are two ways to scale the historical
result, not statistical bounds or forecasts of additional savings.

These are sums of planned-fuel differences on matching historical
revisions. They are neither measured fuel burn nor additional savings
attributable to a proposal service: the airlines made those revisions
without receiving proposals. The simulation also knows the replacement
route retrospectively. A prospective evaluation must obtain feasible
candidates and their estimated delays using only information available
at the time of the proposed decision. The full channel analysis is the subject of a journal extension of
this work.

\section{Conclusions} \label{sec:conclusions}

Flight-plan revisions provide useful training comparisons for predicting
airline route choices. Both routes were filed for the same flight, so
the label has a clear observational basis. With filing-time shortcuts
removed, the model identifies the replacement route in 68.1\,\% of
held-out revisions, 13.1 percentage points above the best tested
single-indicator rule. On the fifth of pairs with the highest confidence,
accuracy reaches 94.9\,\%. The result is therefore useful both as a
ranking and as a way to identify more reliable predictions.

\balance
Adding stay pairs raises accuracy on that smaller test set from
34.8\,\% to 90.0\,\%, while revision accuracy changes from 68.9\,\%
to 68.1\,\%. This demonstrates the importance of representing retained
routes as well as revisions. The next data question is how to build
stronger comparisons for flights that do not revise, with evidence that
the alternative was available and regulation status does not identify
the label.

The retrospective fuel-saving policy illustrates a separate use of the
predictions. At a threshold selected to target 80\,\% precision,
79.2\,\% of selected routes match the subsequent filing, accounting for
20.1\,kt of planned-fuel differences in the quarter. These figures
measure selection among historical route pairs. They motivate research
on proposal acceptance without estimating the additional fuel savings
of a future service.

The two evaluations are complementary: revisions test which replacement
is predicted, while stay pairs test recognition of retained routes.
Neither population alone covers the full route-choice problem.

Route representation is another scientific question. Waypoint identifiers
have the largest individual feature attribution, while removing them and
retraining reduces accuracy by 1.4 points. The representation captures
which published points a route uses, but discards their order and
geometry. Initial experiments with a Transformer encoder of geometric
route descriptors did not improve on the token-based model. Its
embeddings also separated preferred from abandoned routes only slightly
better than chance. Those findings motivate further work on representations
that retain useful network context while depending less on waypoint names.

Finally, future evaluation should test how the model responds to changes
in operating conditions and fuel prices, and compare planned-fuel
differences with flown trajectories. Prospectively recording candidate
availability and airline responses would establish how well the
predictable choices found here transfer to actual proposals.
