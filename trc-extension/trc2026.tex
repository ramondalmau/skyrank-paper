\begin{abstract}
Flight-plan revisions provide direct comparisons for learning route
preferences: a route previously filed for a flight and its replacement.
We use 1.48 million pairs from eighteen months of European traffic to
train a ranking model with route indicators, flight context and waypoint
identifiers. Shared flight attributes prevent message timing from
revealing the label. On a later test quarter, the model identifies the
replacement in 68.1\,\% of cases, compared with 55.0\,\% for the best
single-indicator rule and 57.5\,\% for the strongest logistic baseline.
Accuracy reaches 94.9\,\% on the fifth of pairs with the highest
confidence. Matched examples of retained routes improve predictions on
a smaller stay-pair test set. A retrospective policy then selects
lower-fuel routes above a calibrated probability threshold. At an
operating point targeting 80\,\% precision, 79.2\,\% of selections
match the subsequent filing and account for 20.1\,kilotonnes of
planned-fuel differences over the quarter. These results establish
useful prediction and confidence-based selection from revision records.
The fuel total describes historical choices; acceptance of actual
proposals and additional savings require prospective evaluation.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

On a spring morning in 2026, an Airbus A320 was filed from Amsterdam to
Barcelona on a route carrying 102 minutes of attributed air traffic
flow management (ATFM) delay, and the airline subsequently re-filed.
The new route shed that delay completely, and it cost the airline on
each of the three counts an airline pays for directly: 39 minutes of
flight time, 1\,397\,kg of extra planned fuel, and 466 euros more in
en-route charges. The records show a substantial trade-off between attributed delay and
other route indicators. They do not reveal the airline's internal costs
or all the circumstances behind its decision.

This paper asks whether recorded revisions can be used to predict route
choice. An airline may revise a plan after a delay is attributed, an
inbound aircraft arrives late, a forecast changes, or a route becomes
unavailable. The archive records the earlier route and its replacement,
but does not identify which of these reasons prompted the change. We
use each pair as an observation of filing behaviour: given the two routes
and their characteristics, can a model identify the replacement without
being told their filing order?

That question matters because route indicators do not determine choice
on their own. A lower-fuel route may carry more delay or higher en-route
charges, and the effect of delay depends on the aircraft's subsequent
flights. Published assessments of European network performance also
recognise the role of capacity constraints in route extension
\citep{prc2026}. Predicting a route choice is therefore a different task
from finding the route with the lowest value of one indicator. Here the
aim is to learn patterns in recorded choices, without reconstructing
an airline's private cost function or attributing inefficiency to an
individual operator.

The main methodological contribution is the construction of the training
comparison. Both routes in a revision pair were filed for the same
flight. This provides a direct observation of a replacement, whereas
ranking a filed route above generated alternatives assumes a preference
that the record may not establish. Revisions also present two challenges:
message timing can reveal which route came later, and the sample excludes
flights that kept their routes. We address the first through feature
construction and study the second using matched examples of retained
routes.

The second contribution is an evaluation of prediction reliability.
We test on a later period, compare the model with simple rules and
learned baselines, and examine how accuracy changes when low-confidence
predictions are withheld. Ablations and feature attributions answer
complementary questions: which inputs improve a retrained model, and
which inputs influence the predictions of the fitted model?

The third contribution is a retrospective evaluation of selective
fuel-saving proposals. It asks whether a confidence threshold identifies
lower-fuel routes that agree with subsequent filings, how much planned
fuel those matching choices represent, and how the results vary with the
threshold and stay-pair weighting. This use case illustrates the model's
potential value. It does not measure responses to actual proposals or
additional savings caused by a service.

This manuscript develops the work described in the companion SESAR
Innovation Days 2026 conference paper. The conference version presents
the revision-based method and its main results. This journal version
adds the detailed baseline comparisons, encoding and ablation analysis,
proposal-policy evaluation, and discussion of the conditions under which
recorded choices may inform future proposals.

The remainder of this manuscript is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:background} explains why a revision constitutes a
preference observation and why it carries a stronger label than
generated candidates; Section~\ref{sec:related} positions the work in
the literature. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the archive and pair
construction, Section~\ref{sec:model} the model and its calibration,
Section~\ref{sec:results} predictive performance, and
Section~\ref{sec:channel} the proposal-channel simulation.
Section~\ref{sec:validity} collects the threats to validity, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

This section introduces the filing process, explains what a revision
label records, and distinguishes route-choice prediction from its
potential uses. It also relates the pairwise model to established
choice-modelling methods.

\subsection{Flight-plan revisions as preference data}

In the European network a flight plan is filed before departure and may
be revised at any time until the flight leaves. Every filing is a
message, and so is every later change, and the network keeps them all, so
that what it holds for a flight is the whole sequence of messages sent for
it. A route change is accordingly a pair of consecutive messages rather
than a record of its own: the message that changed the horizontal route of
the plan, and the message that preceded it.

The two messages of a pair together describe a decision, because the airline had committed to the earlier
route and then filed the later one for the same flight on the same day,
which makes the later route the one it chose and the earlier one the route
it abandoned. That the pair
is assembled from two messages sent at different times is not a
detail of bookkeeping: it shapes the encoding, and
Section~\ref{sec:leakage} returns to it.

The sharpest of those decisions, and the ones in which the terms of the
trade are clearest, are made under flow management. When expected traffic exceeds the capacity available in a volume of
airspace, a \emph{regulation} limits the rate at which flights may enter
it. The imbalance can reflect demand or capacity changes associated with
staffing, weather or equipment.
Flights planning to cross the volume receive calculated take-off times
consistent with that rate. If a calculated take-off time is later than
the requested time, the difference is the flight's \emph{attributed ATFM
delay}. The aircraft waits on the ground rather than in the air.
Not every flight crossing a regulated volume is delayed, and a flight
affected by several regulations is attributed to the most penalising one.

An airline facing such a delay has a choice that is genuinely economic:
accept it, or file a route that avoids the constrained volume at the
cost of distance, fuel, en-route charges and the departure slot it
already holds, and neither answer is universally correct. For
instance, a flight with a tight aircraft rotation (i.e., a schedule in
which the same aircraft flies several legs in the day, so that delay
on one leg propagates to the next) may find forty minutes of delay far
more expensive than a tonne of fuel, whereas the same delay on the
last leg of the day may well be cheaper than the detour. The decision
is worth learning rather than deriving, since the trade depends on
airline-specific and flight-specific costs that the network does not
observe: published European reference values \citep{cook2015} give
the order of magnitude of the cost of delay and are widely used for
analysis, but they are not the cost function any particular airline
applies, and what an individual operator would charge itself for forty
minutes is not known outside it.

Most revisions in this archive have no recorded regulation: in
seven pairs in ten neither route carries a regulation, and
Section~\ref{sec:data} reports what that majority looks like. A
dispatcher re-planning after a forecast update, or after the aircraft's
earlier legs have moved, leaves the same kind of trace as one escaping a
delay, and the archive records what changed rather than why.

\subsection{Potential uses of the predictions}
\label{sec:usecases}

One potential use is to screen fuel-saving route proposals. For a flight
with a filed route and a feasible alternative, a preference model could
help identify when the lower-fuel option is also likely to be chosen.
A confidence threshold would allow the system to withhold uncertain
suggestions. Section~\ref{sec:channel} examines that principle on
historical revision pairs, using subsequent filings as the reference.

A second research question concerns voluntary rerouting when demand
exceeds the capacity of an airspace volume. Predicted preferences might
help compare which candidate reroutes are both acceptable to operators
and useful for reducing demand. That question would require evaluating
route choice together with network effects; the present study contains
no overload-release experiment.

For either use, agreement with a historical filing is evidence about
choice prediction, not a measured probability of accepting an unsolicited
proposal. Actual responses would provide a more direct evaluation.
They would also introduce selection bias: only the proposals sent could
receive responses, and confidence filtering would favour proposals predicted to succeed.

\subsection{Revealed preference and the construction of pairs}
\label{sec:pairsvsets}
\label{sec:discrete}

Readers of this journal will recognise the setting as a route-choice
problem, a family with a long history in travel-demand modelling
\citep{mcfadden1974,benakiva1985,train2009}. The observation unit here
is the choice between exactly two alternatives that the decision maker
itself constructed, which avoids generating alternatives for the training labels, and the estimator is a
gradient-boosted ranker rather than a parametric logit.

The pairwise loss has the same logistic form as a binary choice model
applied to the difference between two utility scores. Here the score is
learned with decision trees rather than a linear combination of inputs.
This allows interactions between route characteristics and flight
context, but the fitted scores are predictive quantities: they do not
identify an airline's economic utility or willingness to pay.

The connection to revealed preference is the use of observed behaviour
rather than stated intentions \citep{samuelson1938}. For a revision,
the observed behaviour is precise: the airline replaced one filed route
with another. Interpreting this as a preference between simultaneously
available options is an additional assumption, because conditions may
have changed between filings. Stay pairs, introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:stay}, require a further assumption because their
alternatives come from different flights.

The label construction determines which comparison the model learns.
The authors' earlier route-ranking work aimed to predict plans not yet
filed, allowing traffic demand to be estimated before the filing horizon
\citep{dalmau2024}. It ranked each filed plan above generated alternatives,
using up to eleven route options from the Network Manager, EUROCONTROL's
network-level flow-management function. On the pre-tactical set, none
of those alternatives need match the eventual filing.

With generated alternatives, successful prediction can partly reflect
differences between filed and generated routes. The archive alone does
not tell us whether the airline examined and rejected those alternatives.
This does not make generated routes unsuitable as candidates; it limits
what can be inferred when they are labelled as rejected choices.

A revision offers a more direct comparison
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pairidea}). The airline filed both routes, one after the
other, for the same flight. The label therefore records a replacement
that actually occurred. It does not prove that both routes appeared on
a dispatcher's screen or remained available at the same decision time.
No dataset in this study provides both label constructions for the same
flights, so their relative predictive performance is not measured here.

The model is trained and evaluated on pairs. In principle, a scoring
function could also compare several candidates for one flight. However,
the indicators are normalised relative to the alternatives being scored
(Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). Adding a candidate can change those reference
values and therefore the scores. Extending the method to larger sets
requires evaluating that dependence and checking whether the pairwise
calibration remains appropriate; the present results establish neither.

The price to be paid is that revisions are not a random sample of
flights, but rather flights that changed, which introduces a bias of
its own. Rather than assuming that bias away,
Section~\ref{sec:changebias} measures it.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Three threads of literature meet in this paper, and the first,
rerouting in air traffic flow management, has largely approached the
problem from the network side. Rerouting was formulated as a dynamic
network flow problem \citep{bertsimas2000} and later as a stochastic
extension that reroutes flights as capacity evolves
\citep{mukherjee2009}; both optimise a network objective and assume
the resulting assignment is executed, so the airline's willingness to
fly it is not modelled.

The value of airline collaboration in flow management has been
examined \citep{castelli2012}, which motivates the channel simulated
here but not which proposals would be taken up. Closer still,
incorporating airline preferences into collaborative rerouting changes
which reroutes are selected \citep{murca2018,xu2020}, as does the
collaborative trajectory options programme, where an airline attaches
to each alternative a cost relative to its preferred option
\citep{kulkarni2018}. In all of these the preference is an input the
airline must supply; this paper learns the preference function
itself, at network scale, from what airlines did rather than stated,
providing a predictive estimate that could complement explicitly supplied
preferences.

Other studies estimate preferences from observations rather than asking
airlines to supply them. Historical trajectories have been clustered and
classified to provide options for demand-capacity optimisation
\citep{degiovanni2024}. The probability of a regulated flight rerouting
on its own has also been predicted to help assess the consequences of
a regulation \citep{dalmau2022}. Those tasks concern behaviour without
a proposal. Predicting the response to a particular proposed route is
a related but distinct question.

Airlines also express priorities within a regulation through the
user-driven prioritisation process \citep{pilon2021,ruiz2019}. This
orders delay among an airline's own flights rather than choosing routes.
Explicitly supplied priorities and predicted route preferences could
therefore provide complementary information.

The second thread is economic: earlier than any of the above, route
choice in Europe was studied as a trade between fuel and en-route
charges \citep{delgado2015}, documenting economic influences on choice without using revision pairs, and the discrete-choice tradition of
Section~\ref{sec:discrete} supplies the theoretical frame for such
labels; revisions provide a useful source of paired filing observations, although
simultaneous availability of both routes is not established.

The third thread is methodological: learning a ranking function from
pairs is standard \citep{burges2005,liu2009}, and learning preferences
from logged behaviour rather than elicited judgements was established
in information retrieval, where clicks on search results yield
reliable relative, though not absolute, relevance labels
\citep{joachims2002}. The revision archive is the aviation analogue of
a click log, in that nobody was asked anything and each record must be interpreted in the context in which it arose; the same literature supplies the caution this paper
inherits, that logged data reflects the behaviour of the system that
logged it (Section~\ref{sec:validity}). The direct predecessor of
this work \citep{dalmau2024} established the framing adopted here, a
gradient-boosted ranker scoring routes by their indicators in context,
and reported the difficulty motivating the present paper: its
training pairs rank the filed plan above candidates the airline may
never have examined, the argument and label construction
Section~\ref{sec:pairsvsets} sets out in full.

\subsection{The archive}

Eighteen consecutive months of European network data, from January 2025
to June 2026, are used. After retaining only those revisions that
change the horizontal route, and only those pairs with complete key
performance indicators, the archive contains 1.48 million labelled
pairs. Each pair is one training example containing two route rows and
a label identifying the replacement. Planned fuel refers throughout to
the fuel estimate attached to the recorded plan, not measured fuel burn.
Each route is described by twenty-three features, summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:features}, one of which is in-house shorthand and worth
spelling out: the protected volume is the volume of airspace whose
capacity the regulation was declared to protect.

``Airline'' is used loosely throughout for any aircraft operator,
since the archive contains business, cargo and non-scheduled traffic
as well as scheduled airlines, and the figures say ``operator'' where
the count is over all of them. All the data used is held by the
network for operational purposes and was processed under existing
data-handling arrangements. Operator identity enters the model as a categorical feature only,
and no operator or airspace volume is named anywhere in this paper. A
small number of individual flights are reported as worked examples,

\subsection{Stay pairs}
\label{sec:stay}

Revision pairs describe only flights that changed route. They do not
record the alternatives considered by flights that kept their route.
A model trained on revisions can therefore learn a pattern that is
useful within that sample but favours changes too readily elsewhere.
Section~\ref{sec:changebias} measures this effect on a smaller,
separately constructed set of \emph{stay pairs}.

Each stay pair joins two flights on the same day, with the same origin,
destination and aircraft type. They flew different routes and neither
revised its plan. One flight was regulated and the other was not. We
label the regulated flight's retained route as preferred and use the
other flight's route as its comparison alternative. Both route rows
are given the retained-route flight's context before scoring. The model
therefore compares routes for that flight, not the two flights' scores.

The retained route is observed; rejection of the comparison alternative
is inferred. The other flight demonstrates that its route was used that
day, but not that the regulated flight considered it or could have used
it at its own departure time. The label is consequently weaker than a
revision label. In particular, it cannot distinguish preference for the
retained route from differences in availability or unrecorded constraints.

There are 6\,672 stay pairs, including 1\,093 in the test months,
compared with 1.48 million revision pairs. Stay pairs are split by time
and added to the corresponding revision subsets. During training,
an increased group weight gives each stay pair a larger contribution
to the ranking loss.

The flights that stayed faced a median peak attributed delay of
31 minutes. In 47.5\,\% of stay pairs, the retained route uses more
planned fuel than the comparison route; the median difference is
3\,kg in favour of the retained route. These figures describe retained
routes despite delay and similar planned fuel. They do not, by
themselves, demonstrate a preference for stability or rejection of an
available alternative.

One property of this construction has to be stated alongside it,
because it bounds what the stay pairs can ever be used to measure. The
regulated flight is always the one whose route was kept, so regulation
status identifies the labelled answer. Concretely, in all 1\,093 stay
pairs of the test months the kept route carries a positive attributed
delay and at least one regulation, and the alternative carries neither,
with no exceptions. A rule as crude as ``prefer the route of the flight
that was regulated'' is therefore right on every stay pair without
examining a route at all. Whether the model takes that shortcut is a
question the archive can answer, because it also contains the mirror
population in which the same rule is always wrong;
Section~\ref{sec:changebias} puts the two side by side.

\subsection{Encoding and leakage control}
\label{sec:leakage}

The replacement route comes from the later message. If message timing
were allowed to differ between the two rows, it could reveal the label
without providing any information about route choice. This is called
\emph{label leakage}: the input contains a clue to the answer that would
not be available when predicting a new decision.

One held-out pair illustrates the problem. The original plan was filed
at 00:30 and its replacement at 10:56 for a flight due off the blocks
around 12:30. Time to departure would be 720 minutes for the earlier
message and 94 minutes for the later one. A rule choosing the smaller
value would identify the replacement without comparing the routes.
Across the test quarter, the median interval between filings is
33 minutes, a quarter of pairs are more than 90 minutes apart, and the
replacement is the later message in all but a thousandth of pairs.
The same shortcut would persist in a chronological test set.

We therefore give both rows the same flight-level context, taken from
the later message; time to departure takes the pair minimum. For stay
pairs, the context of the flight that retained its route is copied to
both rows. Route-specific quantities can still differ. One deliberate
exception within the rotation features is outbound connection time:
it is shortened for the slower route by the difference in flight time.
Aligning context removes the direct timing shortcut. It does not
reconstruct both routes' indicators at a common historical decision
instant; that distinction matters for prospective use.

The four route indicators are then expressed relative to the smaller
value in their pair. For indicator $k$ and route $r\in\{A,B\}$,
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde{x}_{rk}=x_{rk}-\min(x_{Ak},x_{Bk}).
\end{equation}
For example, planned-fuel values of 2\,000 and 2\,300\,kg become
0 and 300\,kg. The operation is applied separately to fuel, flight time,
distance and charges; the minimum for each indicator need not belong
to the same route. Units remain unchanged. A zero fuel input means no extra fuel relative
to the comparison route, not zero fuel for the flight. The encoding emphasises the
extra requirement of one route relative to the other, while shared
context still tells the model which flight is being considered.

This normalisation is a representation choice, not another leakage
correction. Absolute fuel use is valid information, but the comparison
encoding performs substantially better in the ablation reported in
Section~\ref{sec:ablation-sec}. It does not impose a common economic value on fuel differences.

\subsection{Splits}
\label{sec:splits}

The evaluation is strictly out of time, in the sense that the model is
fitted on January 2025 through February 2026 (1\,134\,064 pairs),
tuned on March 2026 (67\,349), and evaluated once on April to June 2026
(281\,720), the held-out quarter cited throughout; the three account for
all 1.48 million revision pairs of the archive. These are pair counts:
a flight may contribute several revisions. Stay pairs are assigned to
the corresponding periods and evaluated separately. Every threshold, calibrator and stopping decision is taken on the
tuning month alone, flights appearing in the tuning or evaluation
months are removed from the fitting set, and eleven automated checks
verify split disjointness, feature construction and the pair
invariants.

\section{Model, training and calibration}
\label{sec:model}

This section separates three operations: learning route scores, turning
a score difference into a probability, and choosing a threshold for
selective use. The model follows the predecessor system
\citep{dalmau2024}, with the encoding and stay-pair construction described
above.

The model assigns each route a score $s(x)$ from its encoded features
$x$. The route with the higher score is the predicted choice. Scores have
no physical units and are not probabilities. Training uses pairs with
one route labelled preferred. If their scores are $s_+$ and $s_-$, the
pairwise logistic loss is
\begin{equation}
 \ell=\log\!\left(1+\exp[-(s_+-s_-)]\right).
\end{equation}
The loss is large when the labelled preferred route scores lower.
It decreases smoothly as that route's lead grows, encouraging both the
correct ordering and a larger score gap \citep{burges2005}. A pair's
group weight multiplies its contribution to training. For revisions,
$s_+$ is the replacement's score; for stay pairs, it is the retained
route's score. The plus sign identifies the training label, so $s_+$
can be lower than $s_-$ when the prediction is wrong.

The scoring function uses gradient-boosted decision trees in CatBoost
\citep{prokhorenkova2017}, with depth 8, an automatically selected
learning rate and early stopping on the tuning month. Early stopping
uses the tuning criterion to select how many trees to retain, rather
than continuing training indefinitely. Each successive
tree helps improve the current scores. The trees can represent
interactions: for example, the effect of a route's extra flight time
can depend on the time available before the aircraft's next flight.
Stay-pair weights of 10, 30 and 100 are examined; the adopted value is
$w=10$ (Section~\ref{sec:changebias}).

The route's waypoint identifiers are encoded as a bag of tokens.
Each identifier is treated as a word, and the model receives the
collection of words used by the route. Order and coordinates are
discarded. This representation lets two routes with similar aggregate
indicators differ according to the published points they use. It can
learn associations with those parts of the network, but it does not
explicitly reconstruct the route's geometry or airspace crossings.

After training, the absolute score gap $g=|s_A-s_B|$ measures confidence
in the ordering. Calibration fits a mapping $q(g)$ from that gap to the
probability that the higher-scoring route matches the label. A value
of 0.8 means that about eight in ten comparable predictions are expected
to be correct. It is a statement about a group of predictions, not a
promise about one flight. To fit the mapping, we score the tuning pairs and compare each ordering
with its known label. Their score gaps and correctness indicators provide
the calibration data. The mapping is then fixed for the test quarter
\citep{zadrozny2002}; fitting it does not change the underlying route
scores.

For the worked example, scores of $-2.82$ and $+0.90$ give a gap of
about 3.7, which maps to 0.99. In that test-set gap band, the model is
correct in 97.4\,\% of cases. A gap of about half a point maps to
approximately 0.72, compared with 68.5\,\% observed accuracy in that
band. These comparisons show what calibration means and how it can
overstate confidence on later data.

We compare the raw sigmoid of the gap, Platt scaling (a fitted sigmoid)
and isotonic regression (a stepwise fit constrained not to decrease
with the gap). Isotonic regression
has the lowest tuning-month log loss and is selected before testing.
Log loss rewards accurate probabilities and penalises confident mistakes.
On the held-out quarter, the raw sigmoid has an expected calibration
error of 0.9 percentage points, compared with 1.8 for the selected
isotonic mapping. This error averages the discrepancy between predicted
probabilities and observed frequencies across fifteen bands; it does
not bound every band's discrepancy. Across the coarser reported gap
bands, the largest discrepancy is 4.4 points. Calibration methods can
behave differently across periods and evaluation criteria
\citep{guo2017}; the test result is reported without changing the
selection made on the tuning month.

The proposal policy uses the probability for the lower-fuel route.
If that route has the higher score, its probability is $q(g)$;
otherwise it is $1-q(g)$. A threshold on this quantity determines
which pairs are selected. Threshold and precision are distinct: a
threshold of 0.600 selects candidate probabilities strictly above that value,
whereas precision is the observed fraction correct among all retained
predictions. Section~\ref{sec:channel} selects thresholds for target
precision on the tuning month and measures the resulting precision
on the test quarter.

\section{Predictive performance}
\label{sec:results}

We first compare overall accuracy with simpler methods and examine how
it changes when the model withholds uncertain predictions. We then
report results by decision context, assess the effect of model inputs
and stay-pair training, and inspect errors and feature contributions.

\subsection{Accuracy, coverage and baselines}
\label{sec:heuristics}

On the held-out quarter the model identifies the airline's chosen route
in 68.1\,\% of 281\,720 pairs (95\,\% interval 67.9--68.3, or
67.7--68.6 under city-pair clustering), against the 50\,\% of random
choice. The tuning-month accuracy is 67.5\,\%, close to the test result.
The chronological test provides evidence of performance on later data;
this comparison alone does not rule out overfitting.

Accuracy alone understates operational value, because the model is
informative about its own uncertainty. Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage} reports
accuracy when the model is permitted to abstain on those cases in which
the two routes score close together. Here, coverage means the fraction of route pairs on which a prediction
is retained. It counts pairs, not distinct flights or proposals. On the fifth with
the largest gaps, accuracy reaches 94.9\,\%. This selection ignores
which route uses less fuel. A fuel-saving policy has an additional
condition, so its own coverage and precision must be evaluated separately
(Section~\ref{sec:channel}).

Within-pair normalisation has the largest effect at the screening

This supports presenting the indicators as comparisons between the

The waypoint result changes with model capacity. Removing waypoint
text changes screening accuracy by only $+0.03$ points. At full scale,
retaining it improves accuracy by 1.4 points: 68.2\,\% versus
66.8\,\%. The 68.2\,\% result is a separate run of the adopted
configuration; its tested seeds span 0.17 percentage points. Thus the
small screening model understates the benefit of the text feature.
The experiment does not isolate why, but learning from many rare
waypoint identifiers may require more capacity.

We also explored a name-independent route representation. A neural
encoder received segment descriptors based on bearing changes relative
to the great circle and segment lengths relative to total route length.
These descriptors remove dependence on absolute scale, rotation and
position. Trained with the pairwise objective, the encoder performed
worse alone than the token representation and added only a fraction
of a point alongside it. A linear classifier on its embeddings separated
preferred from abandoned routes only slightly better than chance.
The representation may discard useful network context, but these tests
do not isolate representation, architecture and optimisation effects.
They predate a repair that recovered about a quarter of valid archive
pairs, so only their qualitative ordering is reported here.

The stay-pair evaluation asks a different question: does including
retained routes improve predictions on that constructed population?
With revision-only training, stay-pair accuracy is 34.8\,\%, so the
model favours the comparison alternative in 65.2\,\% of cases.
Adding stay pairs at weight 10 raises accuracy to 90.0\,\%, while
revision accuracy changes from 68.9\,\% to 68.1\,\%. The 0.8-point
revision difference is measured at full scale; the 3.7-point difference
in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} belongs to the reduced screening model.

Regulation status alone cannot explain the two results. In every stay
pair, the labelled preferred route is regulated. In the 30\,953 test
revisions that leave a regulation, the preferred route is unregulated.
Always preferring the regulated route would succeed on all stay pairs
and fail on all these revisions; reversing the rule would reverse the
results. The model reaches 90.0\,\% and 88.0\,\%, respectively.
It therefore uses information beyond that single rule. This comparison
does not rule out other cues associated with how stay pairs were built.

The stay test contains 1\,093 pairs, with a 95\,\% interval of
$[88.1,91.7]$ around its 90.0\,\% accuracy. Its alternatives belong
to different flights and their availability to the retained-route
flight is not established. The result is strong evidence of improvement
on this test construction, while broader performance on non-revising
flights remains to be evaluated.

The weight sweep compares $w\in\{10,30,100\}$. Raising the weight
from 10 to 100 improves stay-pair accuracy by another 7.3 percentage
points and reduces revision accuracy by 1.5 points. The adopted $w=10$ retains most of the stay-pair improvement
while better preserving revision accuracy.

\subsection{Error analysis}

Fortunately, errors concentrate among low-confidence predictions. Only 5.6\,\%
of all errors occur in the quarter of pairs with the largest score
gaps; that share would be 25\,\% if errors were distributed evenly
across confidence levels. Also, 27.7\,\% of errors are near-ties,
defined as gaps smaller than one fourth of the archive's median gap.
These observations explain why withholding low-confidence predictions
improves accuracy.

High-confidence errors still occur. In one held-out case, all cost
indicators and attributed delay favoured the replacement, but the model
ranked the abandoned route higher. This motivates examining route-text
effects and interactions. The direction of the inputs alone does not
establish which feature caused that particular mistake in a nonlinear
model; a local attribution or controlled perturbation would be needed.

Neither these errors nor stay-pair errors are observed rejections of
proposals. The 10.0\,\% stay-pair error rate is an unthresholded
ranking error on constructed pairs. It cannot be read as the rate of
confident suggestions that an airline would reject. The retrospective
proposal policy has its own precision measure, reported in
Section~\ref{sec:channel}.

\subsection{Feature attribution}
\label{sec:weigh}

A ranking model that cannot be interrogated is of little use to an
operational user who is asked to trust it. Shapley attributions were
therefore computed over the held-out quarter: each feature is credited
with the share of the score that is attributable to it, averaged over
every order in which the features could have been supplied to the model
\citep{lundberg2017}. Because the model decides on a comparison rather
than on one route, what is reported here is the \emph{difference}
between a feature's attribution on the two routes of a pair, averaged
in magnitude over the quarter (Fig.~\ref{fig:shap}). For example,
attributions of $+0.3$ and $+0.1$ on the two routes contribute $0.2$ to
their score difference. These values illustrate the calculation; they
are not an additional empirical result. The figure reports the average
absolute size of such differences. That is a ranking
not of what correlates with acceptance, but of what moves this
particular model's decisions.

Waypoint text has the largest individual mean absolute attribution
difference: 0.40 score units, compared with 0.26 for attributed delay
and 0.20 for planned fuel. The four cost indicators together sum to
0.52. These values describe how the fitted model distributes its score
between inputs; they are not causal effects or economic weights placed
on them by airlines.

Time to departure has an attribution of 0.18 even though it is identical
for both routes. This is possible because the trees use interactions.
For example, a fuel or flight-time difference may affect the score
differently close to departure. A shared context feature cannot separate
the routes on its own, but it can change the effect of features that do
differ. Outbound connection time is different: it varies with route flight time (Table~\ref{tab:features}).

Attribution direction adds information beyond magnitude. Lower
attributed delay contributes strongly towards the chosen route's score,
reaching 2.3 points where that route has one hour and three quarters less
delay. Planned-fuel contributions are smaller and spread across more
pairs; their tendency to favour higher-fuel routes reflects the fitted
relationships in a revision-selected dataset. It should not be interpreted
as a general airline preference for burning more fuel.

Removing an individual waypoint identifier and rescoring changes the
score by up to 2.5 points for the most influential identifier. This is
a sensitivity test on the encoded route, not a feasible rerouting
experiment: deleting a token does not generate a valid alternative.
Individual identifiers are withheld in accordance with the study's
neutrality requirements.

Finally, attribution and ablation answer different questions.
Attribution describes the fitted model; ablation retrains a model
without an input. Although waypoint text has the largest individual
attribution, removing it and retraining costs 1.4 accuracy points.
Correlated inputs can partly replace its predictive information after
retraining. The two results therefore support a useful contribution
from route identifiers without implying that they dominate prediction.

\section{Proposal channel}
\label{sec:channel}

This section uses the ranking model to select lower-fuel routes from
historical pairs. It evaluates agreement with the recorded replacement
and sums planned-fuel differences where that agreement occurs. The
purpose is to study confidence-based selection, rather than to estimate
the effect of introducing a proposal service.

\subsection{Policy and operating points}

The policy has three steps. First, keep pairs whose two routes have
different planned fuel. Secondly, take the lower-fuel route as the
candidate and compute its calibrated probability: $q(g)$ if it has the
higher score, and $1-q(g)$ otherwise. Thirdly, select the candidate
only when that probability exceeds $\tau$. Thresholds are chosen on
the tuning month for target precision and applied unchanged to the test
quarter.

A selection is \emph{confirmed} when the lower-fuel candidate is the
route the airline subsequently filed. Precision is the fraction of
selected candidates confirmed in this way. Proposal coverage is the
fraction of eligible pairs selected. Unlike the accuracy--coverage
curve in Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}, this policy always considers the
lower-fuel route, which need not be the model's preferred route.
The two selection rules therefore answer different questions. For
example, a large score gap favouring higher fuel counts as a confident
prediction in Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage} but produces no fuel-saving
proposal. A lower-fuel candidate with a more moderate score gap can
still pass a fixed probability threshold. Thus the 94.9\,\% accuracy
on the most confident fifth and the 79.2\,\% proposal precision are
not evaluations of the same subset.

Of the 281\,720 test pairs, 275\,907 are eligible (97.9\,\%);
only equal-fuel pairs are excluded. In 55.1\,\% of eligible pairs,
the lower-fuel route is the route being abandoned. Always selecting
the lower-fuel route would therefore match 44.9\,\% of subsequent
filings. The model's role is to identify the subset where lower fuel
and the predicted route choice agree.

Filing order is withheld from the policy. It scores the two historical
options without using which is currently filed, so it may select either
the replacement or the abandoned route. This differs from a service
that only proposes a new alternative to the current plan. It also uses
a replacement known from the historical record, rather than discovering
that route prospectively. These choices define the evaluation as selection within observed revision
pairs. For example, if the lower-fuel route is the later filing and passes
the threshold, the selection is confirmed. If the lower-fuel route is
the earlier filing and passes, the selection is incorrect, because the
airline replaced it. Neither result requires a real proposal to have
been sent. The same rule is applied to every eligible pair.

At all reported thresholds ($\tau\geq0.5$), the selected lower-fuel
route must also be the model's higher-scoring route: the lower-scoring
route has probability no greater than one half. Increasing the target
precision from 60\,\% to 90\,\% increases test precision from
65.8\,\% to 88.2\,\%. The share of eligible pairs selected falls
to less than a third of its earlier value, while confirmed planned
fuel decreases by 43\,\%, from 24.3 to 13.8\,kt. The selected
higher-confidence subset therefore retains a substantial share of the
planned-fuel differences.

At the central operating point, $\tau=0.600$ was selected to target
80\,\% precision. It selects 56\,162 pairs over the test quarter,
about one fifth of eligible pairs. Of these selections, 79.2\,\%
match the subsequent filing. For those matches only, we sum the higher
planned-fuel value minus the lower one. The total is 20.1\,kt, or
63.5\,kt of CO$_2$ using 3.16\,kg of CO$_2$ per kilogramme of fuel.
The mean difference is 452\,kg per confirmed selection and the median
is 130\,kg. The distribution is skewed: the largest tenth accounts
for 64\,\% of the total.

These totals concern changes airlines made without receiving proposals.
They quantify the planned-fuel differences associated with choices the
policy correctly identifies, not additional fuel saved by the policy.
They also concern route pairs, not necessarily unique flights; several
revisions may belong to the same flight.

Two illustrative annualisations give 70 to 80\,kt of planned fuel,
or 222 to 254\,kt of CO$_2$. The lower figure applies the
per-eligible-pair rate to an annual eligible volume estimated from the
eighteen-month archive. The upper figure multiplies the quarter by four.
At the 60\,\% target the corresponding planned-fuel range is 85 to
97\,kt. These ranges express two scaling assumptions, not confidence
intervals or forecasts of additional savings.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:validity}

The results establish predictive value on recorded route pairs.
The following questions determine how far that evidence can be extended.

\paragraph{Recorded choice and preference}
A revision records the output of a filing process, which combines
operator objectives, planning software, available information and
constraints. If the earlier route becomes unavailable, replacement is
necessary even if it would otherwise be preferred. The archive does
not identify that share of revisions. Thus the model predicts recorded
choices; it does not separately recover economic utility in the sense
of \citet{samuelson1938}. Studies with availability information could
separate voluntary substitutions from required ones.

\paragraph{Flights that retain their routes}
Stay-pair training raises accuracy from 34.8\,\% to 90.0\,\% on
1\,093 held-out constructed pairs. Together with 88.0\,\% accuracy
on revisions leaving a regulation, this rules out a fixed rule based
only on regulation status. It does not eliminate all construction
artefacts. The alternative belongs to another flight, and its
availability and consideration are unknown. A stronger dataset would
record alternatives available to the same flight and include retained
routes across different regulation contexts. This would also help
distinguish retention by preference from retention due to other
constraints or lack of reconsideration.

\paragraph{Candidate availability and timing}
The evaluation obtains the alternative from the subsequent filing.
A prospective system would have to obtain it from another source and
check its feasibility at the decision time. Historical routes and
generated routes are both possible sources, but neither guarantees
current availability or the same distribution of inputs seen during
training. Harmonising flight context removes a direct timing clue;
it does not reconstruct what all indicators would have been when
both options were simultaneously assessed. In particular, the
alternative's attributed delay is observed here after it was filed.
Before filing, that delay would need to be estimated against the
current regulation state. Errors in that estimate could affect the
predicted preference.

\paragraph{Route representation and transfer}
The waypoint encoding retains identifiers but omits their order and
coordinates. The 1.4-point full-scale ablation measures their contribution
in this dataset, not how much would transfer to another region or a
changed route network. The initial geometric-encoder experiments did
not improve the adopted representation. Further experiments could
separate the value of geometry, connectivity and regional context,
with evaluation across time and networks. Larger candidate sets also
need separate validation because they change the reference values
used in normalisation.

\paragraph{Planned fuel and additional savings}
The 20.1\,kt total sums planned-fuel differences on correctly selected
historical revisions. Actual fuel burn can differ because of winds,
cruise levels, tactical routing and other operating conditions. The
total is therefore neither a measured saving nor a proven upper bound
on one. Matching plans to flown trajectories would test how closely
the planned differences correspond to actual fuel differences.
Separately, those revisions occurred without the policy. Estimating
additional benefit requires a prospective comparison that can
establish what would have happened without a proposal.

\paragraph{Changes in prices and behaviour}
Fuel enters the model in kilogrammes and route charges in euros.
Their relative economic importance may change with fuel prices and
other conditions. Price-aware features are a testable extension;
converting units alone would not guarantee stable preferences.
Evaluation over longer periods would also help distinguish changes
in input distributions from changes in filing behaviour.

\paragraph{Observing proposal responses}
A prospective study could record feasible candidates, the information
available when they are scored, and operator responses to proposals.
It should distinguish predictive accuracy, proposal acceptance and
incremental benefit as separate outcomes. Confidence-based selection
also determines which responses are observed, so evaluating the
resulting feedback requires accounting for that selection. These
questions follow directly from the promising retrospective results
and define the evidence needed for a different decision setting.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Flight-plan revisions provide useful comparisons for learning airline
route-choice behaviour. Their central advantage is the observational
link between the two alternatives: both were filed for the same flight.
After removing direct filing-time clues, the model identifies the
replacement in 68.1\,\% of held-out revisions. This exceeds the best
tested single-indicator rule at 55.0\,\% and the strongest logistic
baseline at 57.5\,\%. Accuracy rises to 94.9\,\% on the fifth of
pairs with the highest confidence, showing that the model can also
identify predictions that are more reliable.

The experiments clarify which design choices support that result.
Within-pair indicator normalisation has the largest effect in the
screening ablation. Adding stay pairs raises accuracy on the constructed
retained-route test from 34.8\,\% to 90.0\,\%, with a 0.8-point
reduction on revisions. Waypoint text provides a further 1.4 points
at full scale. Its high feature attribution and smaller ablation
cost describe complementary aspects of a model whose inputs share
predictive information.

The retrospective proposal policy demonstrates selective use of those
predictions. At a threshold chosen to target 80\,\% precision,
79.2\,\% of selected lower-fuel routes match the subsequent filing,
compared with 44.9\,\% if the lower-fuel option is always selected.
The matching choices account for 20.1\,kt of planned-fuel differences
over the quarter. That total is stable to the stay-pair training choice
at the tested thresholds. It describes historical choices successfully
identified by the model, rather than savings caused by new proposals.

The next scientific steps are to strengthen the observations for
retained routes, test candidates available at a common decision time,
and examine representations that generalise across route networks and
changing operating conditions. Matching planned and flown trajectories
would clarify the fuel interpretation. A prospective study of operator
responses would then assess whether the predictive information found
in revision records transfers to actual proposal acceptance.
